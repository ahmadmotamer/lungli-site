Spanish–English language exchange: find a partner and practise both

By the Lungli team · Published 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

Spanish and English are one of the most natural pairs for a language exchange: millions of Spanish speakers are learning English, and millions of English speakers are learning Spanish. This guide shows how to find a partner, which Spanish to choose, and the mistakes each of you will help the other fix.

Who you can meet

  • If you speak English and learn Spanish, your partners are in Spain, Mexico, Colombia, Argentina, Chile, Peru and the rest of Latin America, and in Spanish-speaking communities in the United States.
  • If you speak Spanish and learn English, look for partners in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and Ireland who are learning Spanish for work, travel or family.

Time zones help this pair: the Americas share most of their day with the United States and Canada, and Spain is one hour ahead of the United Kingdom.

Which Spanish? Spain or Latin America

Both are the same language, and a speaker from Madrid and one from Bogotá understand each other without effort. The differences are in a few words and forms:

Choose the Spanish of the place you will travel to, work with or have family in. If you have no reason to prefer one, either is a good start — say which one you want in your profile, and your partner will correct you towards it.

Mistakes Spanish speakers often make in English

  • "I have 25 years" instead of "I am 25" — in Spanish you *have* years (*tengo 25 años*); in English you *are* an age.
  • "Is raining" instead of "It is raining" — English always needs a subject, even when Spanish leaves it out.
  • "People is" instead of "people are" — *people* is plural in English, *la gente* is singular.
  • "Actually" used for *actualmente*. In English, *actually* means "in fact"; *actualmente* is "currently".
  • "Explain me" instead of "explain to me".

Mistakes English speakers often make in Spanish

  • Ser and estar. Both mean "to be". *Soy aburrido* means "I am boring"; *estoy aburrido* means "I am bored".
  • "Estoy embarazada" for "I am embarrassed". It means "I am pregnant" — the word you want is *avergonzado* or *me da vergüenza*.
  • "Realizar" for "to realise". *Realizar* means "to carry out"; "I realised" is *me di cuenta*.
  • Por and para. Both can mean "for". *Para* points to a goal or a destination, *por* to a cause, a way or an exchange — *gracias por todo*.
  • Gender. Every noun is masculine or feminine, and the words around it change with it: *la casa blanca*, *el coche blanco*.

A first message that gets an answer

Say who you are, what you offer and ask one real question. Writing it in the language your partner speaks shows effort:

  • In Spanish, from an English speaker: "Hola, me llamo Tom y soy de Leeds. Estoy aprendiendo español y veo que tú aprendes inglés. ¿Te gustaría practicar conmigo? Puedo ayudarte con tu inglés."
  • In English, from a Spanish speaker: "Hi, I am Lucía from Valencia. I am learning English and I see you are learning Spanish. Would you like to practise together? I can help you with your Spanish."

A 30-minute session for this pair

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Greetings and news in Spanish
5–15One topic in Spanish; the Spanish speaker corrects the two or three mistakes that matter most
15–25The same topic in English; the English speaker corrects
25–30Each of you writes down the words and corrections to review

Keep the halves equal. The exchanges that last are the ones where both people feel they learn.

Topics that work well for Spanish and English

  • Food: tapas, tacos, arepas — and what people eat for breakfast where you live.
  • Football and series: easy to start, and full of everyday words.
  • How people talk: the slang of Mexico, Spain or Argentina, and its English versions.
  • Travel plans: where your partner would take you in their country, and why.
