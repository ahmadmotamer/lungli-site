Chinese–English language exchange: find a partner and practise both

By the Lungli team · Published 17 November 2026 · 4 min read

Mandarin Chinese and English are among the most learned languages in the world, and each is the other's hardest partner: tones and characters on one side, tenses and articles on the other. That is why a real partner helps so much. This guide shows how to find one, which Chinese to choose, and the mistakes each of you will help the other fix.

Who you can meet

  • If you speak English and learn Chinese, your partners are in mainland China, Taiwan, Singapore and Malaysia, and in Chinese-speaking communities around the world.
  • If you speak Chinese and learn English, look for partners in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia who are learning Mandarin.

Time zones are the main challenge for this pair: Beijing is eight hours ahead of London in winter and thirteen ahead of New York. An evening in China is a morning in Europe — agree a time that suits both of you before you start.

Which Chinese? Mainland China or Taiwan

Both speak Mandarin and understand each other easily. The writing and some words differ:

Mainland ChinaTaiwan
CharactersSimplified: 学习Traditional: 學習
How learners write soundsPinyin (xuéxí)Zhuyin, also called bopomofo
A taxi出租车計程車

Hong Kong speaks Cantonese and writes traditional characters; if Cantonese is what you want, say so in your profile, since most partners will offer Mandarin.

Mistakes Chinese speakers often make in English

  • Tenses: "Yesterday I go to the shop" instead of "Yesterday I went" — Chinese shows time with words like *昨天*, not by changing the verb.
  • "He" and "she" mixed up — both are *tā* when spoken, so the choice in English is easy to miss.
  • Plurals: "two book" instead of "two books".
  • "Open the computer" instead of "turn on the computer" (*打开电脑*).
  • "I very like it" instead of "I really like it" (*我很喜欢*).

Mistakes English speakers often make in Chinese

  • Tones. *Mā* (mother), *má* (hemp), *mǎ* (horse) and *mà* (to scold) are different words. Ask your partner to correct your tones, not only your words.
  • Measure words. Chinese needs a word between a number and a noun: *一本书* (a book), *一个人* (a person), *一杯茶* (a cup of tea).
  • "是" with adjectives: *我是高兴* is wrong; say *我很高兴* (I am happy).
  • Word order of time: time comes before the verb — *我明天去*, not *我去明天*.
  • 了 everywhere. It does not simply mean "past"; let your partner show you when it is natural.

A first message that gets an answer

Say who you are, what you offer and ask one real question. Writing it in the language your partner speaks shows effort:

  • In Chinese, from an English speaker: "你好！我叫汤姆，来自利兹。我在学中文，看到您在学英语。您愿意和我一起练习吗？我可以帮您练习英语。"
  • In English, from a Chinese speaker: "Hello, I am Li Na from Chengdu. I am learning English and I see you are learning Chinese. Would you like to practise together? I can help you with your Chinese."

A 30-minute session for this pair

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Greetings and news in Chinese
5–15One topic in Chinese; the Chinese speaker corrects tones and the two or three mistakes that matter most
15–25The same topic in English; the English speaker corrects
25–30Each of you writes down the words and corrections to review

Topics that work well for Chinese and English

  • Characters: pick one a day and let your partner tell its story — *休* is a person resting by a tree.
  • Food: what people eat in your partner's province, and the names of dishes.
  • Festivals: the Spring Festival and the Mid-Autumn Festival, and the holidays on your side.
  • Apps and daily life: how people pay, travel and shop in each country.
