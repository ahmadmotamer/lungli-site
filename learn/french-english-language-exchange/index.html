French–English language exchange: find a partner and practise both

By the Lungli team · Published 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

French and English share thousands of words, which makes each easy to start and easy to get wrong. A partner who speaks the other language catches the false friends a course misses. This guide shows how to find one, which French to choose, and the mistakes each of you will help the other fix.

Who you can meet

  • If you speak English and learn French, your partners are in France, Belgium, Switzerland, Quebec, and the many French-speaking countries of West and North Africa.
  • If you speak French and learn English, look for partners in the United Kingdom, Ireland, the United States, Canada and Australia who are learning French for work, study or travel.

Time zones make this pair easy: France and Belgium are one hour ahead of the United Kingdom, and Quebec shares the day with the east of the United States.

Which French? France, Quebec or Africa

They are one language with different accents and some different words:

FranceQuebec
A carune voitureune voiture, or *un char* in casual speech
The weekendle week-endla fin de semaine
An emailun mail or un e-mailun courriel
To shopfaire du shoppingmagasiner

Choose the French of the place you will live in, work with or travel to. Say it in your profile, and your partner will correct you towards it.

Mistakes French speakers often make in English

  • "I am agree" instead of "I agree" — *agree* is a verb on its own (*je suis d'accord*).
  • "Since three years" instead of "for three years" — *depuis* with a length of time is *for*.
  • "Actually" for *actuellement*, and "eventually" for *éventuellement*. *Actuellement* is "currently"; *éventuellement* is "possibly".
  • "Informations" and "advices" — in English, *information* and *advice* have no plural.
  • The "th" sound, which often comes out as "z" or "s": *zis* for *this*. Place the tongue between the teeth.

Mistakes English speakers often make in French

  • Tu and vous. *Tu* is for friends, family and children; *vous* for anyone else, and for more than one person. Start with *vous* and let your partner suggest *tu*.
  • "Je suis plein" for "I am full" after a meal. It can sound vulgar; say *j'ai bien mangé* or *je n'ai plus faim*.
  • "Je suis excité" for "I am excited". It often means something else; say *j'ai hâte* or *je suis impatient*.
  • Gender. *Le* or *la* changes the words around it: *un grand jardin*, *une grande maison*.
  • Silent letters. Most final consonants are not said: *ils parlent* sounds like *il parle*.

A first message that gets an answer

Say who you are, what you offer and ask one real question. Writing it in the language your partner speaks shows effort:

  • In French, from an English speaker: "Bonjour, je m'appelle Tom et je viens de Leeds. J'apprends le français et je vois que vous apprenez l'anglais. Voulez-vous pratiquer avec moi ? Je peux vous aider en anglais."
  • In English, from a French speaker: "Hello, I am Camille from Lyon. I am learning English and I see you are learning French. Would you like to practise together? I can help you with your French."

A 30-minute session for this pair

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Greetings and news in French
5–15One topic in French; the French speaker corrects the two or three mistakes that matter most
15–25The same topic in English; the English speaker corrects
25–30Each of you writes down the words and corrections to review

Topics that work well for French and English

  • False friends: make a list together — *librairie* is a bookshop, *location* is a rental.
  • Food and cooking: a recipe from each of your countries, explained in the other language.
  • Films and series, with the French or English title and why it changed.
  • Work and study: how a working day looks in your city.
