German–English language exchange: find a partner and practise both

By the Lungli team · Published 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

Many German speakers already speak good English and want it to sound natural, and many English speakers learn German for study and work in Germany, Austria and Switzerland. A partner on the other side gives each of you what a course cannot: everyday speech, and honest corrections. This guide shows how to find one, which German to expect, and the mistakes each of you will help the other fix.

Who you can meet

  • If you speak English and learn German, your partners are in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, and in Liechtenstein, Luxembourg and South Tyrol.
  • If you speak German and learn English, look for partners in the United Kingdom, Ireland, the United States, Canada and Australia who are learning German.

Time zones: Germany, Austria and Switzerland are one hour ahead of the United Kingdom and six ahead of New York.

Which German? Germany, Austria or Switzerland

Standard German is written and understood everywhere. What changes is the accent and a few words:

TomatoTomateParadeiserTomate
JanuaryJanuarJännerJanuar

If you will live in Switzerland, ask your partner about Swiss German early; otherwise, learn Standard German and enjoy the regional words as they come.

Mistakes German speakers often make in English

  • "Become" for *bekommen*. *Bekommen* means "to get": "I get a present", not "I become a present".
  • "Since three years" instead of "for three years" — *seit* with a length of time is *for*.
  • "Handy" for a mobile phone. In English *handy* means "useful"; the phone is a mobile or a cell phone.
  • "I am here since Monday" instead of "I have been here since Monday" — English uses the present perfect for something that started in the past and continues.
  • "Make a photo" instead of "take a photo" (*ein Foto machen*).

Mistakes English speakers often make in German

  • Der, die, das. Every noun has a gender, and it is best learned with the noun: *der Tisch*, *die Lampe*, *das Buch*.
  • Du and Sie. *Sie* is the polite "you" for strangers and at work; *du* for friends and family. Start with *Sie* and let your partner offer *du*.
  • The verb at the end. In a sentence with *weil* or *dass*, the verb moves to the end: *ich lerne Deutsch, weil ich in Berlin arbeite*.
  • "Gift" for a present. In German, *das Gift* is poison; a present is *ein Geschenk*.
  • "Bekommen", the same trap in the other direction.

A first message that gets an answer

Say who you are, what you offer and ask one real question. Writing it in the language your partner speaks shows effort:

  • In German, from an English speaker: "Hallo, ich heiße Tom und komme aus Leeds. Ich lerne Deutsch und sehe, dass Sie Englisch lernen. Möchten Sie mit mir üben? Ich kann Ihnen mit Ihrem Englisch helfen."
  • In English, from a German speaker: "Hello, I am Jonas from Hamburg. I am learning English and I see you are learning German. Would you like to practise together? I can help you with your German."

A 30-minute session for this pair

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Greetings and news in German
5–15One topic in German; the German speaker corrects the two or three mistakes that matter most
15–25The same topic in English; the English speaker corrects
25–30Each of you writes down the words and corrections to review

Topics that work well for German and English

  • Long words: build a German compound together and take it apart — *Handschuh*, "hand shoe", is a glove.
  • Work and study: how a working day, an internship or a university course looks in each country.
  • Travel: trains, cities and what to see in your partner's region.
  • Food and seasons: Christmas markets, summer barbecues, and the words that go with them.
