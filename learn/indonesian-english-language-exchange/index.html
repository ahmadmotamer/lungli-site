Indonesian–English language exchange: find a partner and practise both

By the Lungli team · Published 17 November 2026 · 4 min read

Indonesia has one of the largest populations in the world and a huge number of English learners, and Indonesian is one of the friendliest languages for an English speaker to start: no tenses to conjugate, no genders, and the Latin alphabet. This guide shows how to find a partner, how everyday Indonesian differs from the textbook, and the mistakes each of you will help the other fix.

Who you can meet

  • If you speak English and learn Indonesian, most of your partners will be in Indonesia; speakers of Malay in Malaysia, Singapore and Brunei understand much of it too.
  • If you speak Indonesian and learn English, look for partners in Australia, the United States, the United Kingdom and Canada who are learning Indonesian — Australia especially, where it is taught in many schools.

Time zones: Jakarta is seven hours ahead of London in winter and three hours behind Sydney.

Textbook Indonesian and everyday Indonesian

Courses teach the formal language; people chat in a relaxed one. You will meet both:

FormalEverydayEnglish
sayaaku (gue in Jakarta)I
tidaknggaknot, no
bagaimanagimanahow
sudahudahalready

Start with the formal words — they are correct everywhere — and ask your partner to show you the everyday ones as they come up.

Mistakes Indonesian speakers often make in English

  • Tenses: "Yesterday I go to the market" instead of "Yesterday I went" — Indonesian shows time with words like *kemarin*, not by changing the verb.
  • "He" and "she" mixed up — Indonesian has one word, *dia*, for both.
  • "I am already eat" instead of "I have already eaten" (*saya sudah makan*).
  • Missing "-s": "She work in Jakarta" instead of "She works".
  • Missing "a" and "the": Indonesian has no articles.

Mistakes English speakers often make in Indonesian

  • Adjectives after the noun: *rumah besar* is "a big house"; *besar rumah* is not.
  • Prefixes and suffixes. One root makes many words: *baca* (read), *membaca* (to read), *bacaan* (reading material). They are easier with a partner than from a list.
  • Anda and kamu. *Anda* is polite and safe with strangers; *kamu* is for friends. Older people are often called *Bapak* or *Ibu*.
  • The letter c, which sounds like "ch": *cari* (to look for) is "chari".
  • Translating "is": *Dia guru* means "He is a teacher" — no extra word for "is" is needed.

A first message that gets an answer

Say who you are, what you offer and ask one real question. Writing it in the language your partner speaks shows effort:

  • In Indonesian, from an English speaker: "Halo, nama saya Tom dari Leeds. Saya sedang belajar bahasa Indonesia, dan saya lihat Anda sedang belajar bahasa Inggris. Apakah Anda mau berlatih bersama saya? Saya bisa membantu bahasa Inggris Anda."
  • In English, from an Indonesian speaker: "Hello, I am Putri from Bandung. I am learning English and I see you are learning Indonesian. Would you like to practise together? I can help you with your Indonesian."

A 30-minute session for this pair

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Greetings and news in Indonesian
5–15One topic in Indonesian; the Indonesian speaker corrects the two or three mistakes that matter most
15–25The same topic in English; the English speaker corrects
25–30Each of you writes down the words and corrections to review

Topics that work well for Indonesian and English

  • Islands and travel: Bali, Java, Sumatra, Sulawesi — where your partner would take you, and why.
  • Food: nasi goreng, rendang, sate — and what people eat for breakfast on your side.
  • Everyday slang: the words in your partner's messages that no textbook has.
  • Music and series: what people are watching and listening to this month.
