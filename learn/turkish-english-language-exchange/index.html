Turkish–English language exchange: find a partner and practise both

By the Lungli team · Published 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

Turkish and English are built in very different ways, which is exactly why a partner helps: each of you can explain what the other's textbook makes look strange. This guide shows how to find a partner, how Turkish words are built, and the mistakes each of you will help the other fix.

Who you can meet

  • If you speak English and learn Turkish, your partners are in Turkey and Northern Cyprus, and in the large Turkish-speaking communities of Germany, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom.
  • If you speak Turkish and learn English, look for partners in the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada and Australia who are learning Turkish for travel, work or family.

Time zones: Istanbul is three hours ahead of London in winter and two in summer, so an evening in Turkey is late afternoon in the United Kingdom.

How Turkish builds a word

Turkish adds endings one after another, where English uses separate words. One example says more than a rule:

TurkishEnglish
eva house
evlerhouses
evlerimmy houses
evlerimdein my houses
evlerimdeyizwe are in my houses

Once you see the pattern, long words become easy to read: take the endings off one at a time.

Mistakes Turkish speakers often make in English

  • "He" and "she" mixed up — Turkish has one word, *o*, for both, so the choice in English is easy to forget in a fast conversation.
  • Missing "a" and "the" — Turkish has no articles: "I bought car" instead of "I bought a car".
  • The verb at the end: "I English learn" instead of "I learn English". English puts the verb after the subject.
  • "W" and "v" — *wine* and *vine* can sound the same; round the lips for "w".
  • "I am living here since 2020" instead of "I have lived here since 2020" — English uses the present perfect for something that started in the past and continues.

Mistakes English speakers often make in Turkish

  • Vowel harmony. An ending changes its vowel to match the word: *evler* (houses) but *kitaplar* (books).
  • I and ı. The dotted *i* and the dotless *ı* are different letters and different words: *kir* is dirt, *kır* is the countryside.
  • The verb at the end: *Ben Türkçe öğreniyorum* — "I Turkish am learning".
  • Sen and siz. *Sen* is for friends and family; *siz* for strangers, older people and more than one person. Start with *siz*.
  • Too many words. "In my house" is one word, *evimde*. Learners often try to translate each English word separately.

A first message that gets an answer

Say who you are, what you offer and ask one real question. Writing it in the language your partner speaks shows effort:

  • In Turkish, from an English speaker: "Merhaba, adım Tom, Leeds'liyim. Türkçe öğreniyorum ve sizin İngilizce öğrendiğinizi görüyorum. Benimle pratik yapmak ister misiniz? İngilizcenize yardımcı olabilirim."
  • In English, from a Turkish speaker: "Hello, I am Elif from Izmir. I am learning English and I see you are learning Turkish. Would you like to practise together? I can help you with your Turkish."

A 30-minute session for this pair

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Greetings and news in Turkish
5–15One topic in Turkish; the Turkish speaker corrects the two or three mistakes that matter most
15–25The same topic in English; the English speaker corrects
25–30Each of you writes down the words and corrections to review

Topics that work well for Turkish and English

  • Everyday phrases with no English twin: *afiyet olsun*, *kolay gelsin*, *geçmiş olsun* — when people say them, and what you would say in English.
  • Food and tea: breakfast in Turkey, and what people drink at work.
  • Series: Turkish series are watched around the world — a good shared topic.
  • Cities: Istanbul, Izmir, Ankara, and your partner's home town.
