Arabic–English language exchange: find a partner and practise both

By the Lungli team · Published 17 November 2026 · 5 min read

Arabic speakers learning English and English speakers learning Arabic need each other: a textbook cannot teach the Arabic people really speak, or the English they really write. This guide shows how to find a partner, which Arabic to choose, and the mistakes each of you will help the other fix.

Who you can meet

  • If you speak English and learn Arabic, your partners are in Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the Gulf, Jordan, Lebanon, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Iraq, and in Arabic-speaking communities around the world.
  • If you speak Arabic and learn English, look for partners in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia who are learning Arabic for study, work, faith or family.

Time zones are kind to this pair in the evening: Cairo is two hours ahead of London in winter, and the Gulf is three or four.

Which Arabic? Standard Arabic or a dialect

This is the most important choice for an English speaker, and it is worth saying in your profile.

Modern Standard Arabic (فصحى)A dialect (عامية)
Where you meet itNews, books, signs, formal speechesEveryday conversation, films, songs
Who speaks it at homeNobody, as a first languageEverybody, in their own region
Most widely understoodAcross the whole Arab world, in writingEgyptian, thanks to films and television
Good forReading, writing, the Quran, studyTalking to people, travel, friends

Many learners start with Standard Arabic and add one dialect. If you will live in or visit one country, choose its dialect — Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf or Moroccan — and ask your partner to speak it with you.

Mistakes Arabic speakers often make in English

  • "He teacher" instead of "He is a teacher" — Arabic needs no verb "to be" in the present; English always does, and needs *a* or *an* too.
  • "Open the light" instead of "turn on the light" — you open and close doors; you *turn on* and *turn off* lights.
  • "I am going to the work every day" instead of "I go to work every day" — English uses the simple present for habits, and no *the* before *work*, *school* or *home*.
  • "P" and "b" — *park* and *bark* can sound the same, because Arabic has no "p". Practise the puff of air on "p".
  • "Since five years" instead of "for five years" — *منذ* is *for* with a length of time.

Mistakes English speakers often make in Arabic

  • The sounds English does not have: ع (ʿayn), ح (ḥā'), خ (khā') and ق (qāf). A partner who says the word slowly, again and again, helps more than any recording.
  • Speaking to a man or a woman. Arabic changes "you" and the verb: *أنتَ تتكلم* to a man, *أنتِ تتكلمين* to a woman.
  • Mixing Standard Arabic and a dialect in one sentence. It sounds odd to a native speaker; ask your partner to tell you which words belong where.
  • Reading without the short vowels. Most Arabic is written without them. Ask your partner to add them (تشكيل) to new words at first.

A first message that gets an answer

Say who you are, what you offer and ask one real question. Writing it in the language your partner speaks shows effort:

  • In Arabic, from an English speaker: "مرحبًا، اسمي توم من ليدز. أتعلم اللغة العربية، وأرى أنك تتعلم الإنجليزية. هل تود أن نتدرب معًا؟ يمكنني مساعدتك في الإنجليزية."
  • In English, from an Arabic speaker: "Hello, I am Sara from Cairo. I am learning English and I see you are learning Arabic. Would you like to practise together? I can help you with your Arabic."

A 30-minute session for this pair

MinutesWhat you do
0–5Greetings in Arabic — there are many, and each has its answer
5–15One topic in Arabic; the Arabic speaker corrects the two or three mistakes that matter most
15–25The same topic in English; the English speaker corrects
25–30Each of you writes down the words and corrections to review

Topics that work well for Arabic and English

  • Greetings and their answers: *السلام عليكم — وعليكم السلام*, *صباح الخير — صباح النور*.
  • Food and family meals, and what changes in Ramadan.
  • Films and songs: Egyptian cinema is one of the easiest ways into a dialect.
  • Daily life: the working week, which in many Arab countries runs from Sunday to Thursday.
