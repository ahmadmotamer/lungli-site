How to correct mistakes in a language exchange

Correcting each other is the heart of a language exchange — and the easiest part to get wrong. Correct too little and nobody learns; correct too much, or too sharply, and conversations stop. Here is how to give corrections that help, and how to receive them.

Agree first

At the start, ask your partner a simple question: "How much would you like me to correct you?" Some people want every mistake; most prefer only the ones that matter. Some like corrections during the conversation; others prefer them at the end. Agreeing first means nobody feels attacked later.

What to correct

You do not need to fix everything. Focus on:

  • Mistakes that change the meaning — when you are not sure what your partner meant, or someone else might misunderstand.
  • Mistakes that come back — the same error in several messages is worth pointing out once, clearly.
  • Things that are correct but unnatural — "It is a good idea" is fine; "Good idea!" is what people actually say.

Leave small slips alone, especially when your partner is trying something difficult. Fluency grows by trying.

How to correct well

  • Show the natural version. "I went to London last year" teaches more than "wrong tense".
  • Add a short reason when you can. "'Last year' is finished, so English uses the simple past." One line is enough.
  • Name the kind of mistake. Grammar, vocabulary, spelling, word choice — it helps your partner see patterns.
  • Keep the conversation going. Correct, then answer what they said. The message mattered, not just its grammar.
  • Be kind. A smile, a "nearly!", or "this is a common one" makes all the difference.

A sharp correction and a kind one

✕ "Wrong. It is went, not have went."

✓ "I went to London last year 🙂 — 'last year' is finished, so English uses the simple past. Did you like London?"

The second says the same thing, explains it, and keeps the conversation alive.

Receiving corrections

  • Say thank you. It encourages your partner to keep helping.
  • Do not argue with the native speaker about what sounds natural — but do ask why, if you are curious.
  • Write the correction down, with the whole sentence. Reviewing it the next day is what makes it stick.
  • Notice your patterns. If the same kind of mistake keeps coming back, that is what to study.
