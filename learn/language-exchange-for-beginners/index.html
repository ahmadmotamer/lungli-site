Language exchange for beginners: can you start with only a few words?

Yes. You do not need to be good at a language to start a language exchange — you need a partner who is patient, a few simple phrases, and a plan for the first conversations.

Why beginners worry, and why they should not

Most people wait until they "know enough" before they talk to a native speaker. That day rarely comes. Grammar books and apps teach you to recognise a language; only real conversation teaches you to use it. A beginner in a language exchange learns the words they actually need, in the order they need them.

Remember that your partner is a learner too. They are practising your language, and they know exactly how it feels to search for a word. Nobody in a good exchange expects you to be fluent.

What you can do with almost no words

Even at the very start, you can:

  • Introduce yourself: your name, where you live, what you do, why you are learning.
  • Ask simple questions: "What do you do at the weekend?", "What is your city like?"
  • Ask about the language: "How do you say...?", "Is this correct?", "What does this word mean?"
  • Use your own language as a safety net: when you are stuck, switch, then try again.

Write these down before your first conversation and keep them in front of you. Reading them out is not cheating — it is how everyone starts.

Start in writing

Writing gives you time. You can think, look a word up and check your sentence before you send it. Your partner can show you a more natural way to say it, and the correction stays on the screen for you to read again. For a beginner, a written exchange is the gentlest start there is.

Split the time, and keep it short

Agree to use one language for the first half and the other for the second. That way you always get your turn to practise, and you always have a turn to rest. Short, frequent exchanges work better than long ones: fifteen minutes three times a week beats one hour on a Sunday.

Ask to be corrected — kindly

Tell your partner what you want: "Please correct my mistakes, but only the important ones." Too many corrections at once are discouraging; none at all means you will keep the same mistakes. One or two per message is plenty for a beginner.

Keep what you learn

Every exchange gives you a few new words and one or two corrections. If you write them down and look at them before the next conversation, you will feel your progress within weeks.
