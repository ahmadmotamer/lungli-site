How to practise English with native speakers online — for free

Textbooks and apps can teach you a lot of English, but there is one thing they cannot give you: a real person who answers. Speaking and writing to native speakers is how your English starts to sound natural. The good news is that you do not need to pay a tutor or live abroad to do it.

Why native speakers make such a difference

  • You hear English as it is really used — short forms, idioms and the words textbooks leave out.
  • You get honest feedback. A native speaker notices at once when a sentence is correct but sounds strange.
  • You learn the culture with the language: what is polite, what is funny, what people actually talk about.
  • You build confidence. After a few real conversations, the fear of speaking gets much smaller.

The free way: a language exchange

The simplest free way to practise English with native speakers is a language exchange. You find someone whose first language is English and who is learning a language you speak — Arabic, Spanish, Portuguese, Turkish, anything. You help them with your language; they help you with English. Nobody pays, because you both give the same thing.

There are millions of English speakers learning other languages, so if you speak almost any language, someone is looking for you.

How to make the most of it

  • Write first, then talk. Writing gives you time to think, and your partner can correct you exactly. When you feel ready, move on to voice.
  • Ask to be corrected — but not on everything. Agree that your partner corrects the mistakes that matter most, with a short explanation.
  • Keep a list of what you learn. New words, better ways to say things, and the mistakes you keep making. Review it before your next conversation.
  • Talk about real things. Your work, your city, a series you are watching. You remember words better when they mean something to you.
  • Help them back generously. The best exchanges are fair: half the time in English, half in your language.

Mistakes English learners often make in conversation

  • "I have went" instead of "I went" — with a finished time like *yesterday* or *last year*, English uses the simple past.
  • "I am agree" instead of "I agree" — *agree* is a verb on its own.
  • "He said me" instead of "he told me" or "he said to me".
  • "Since three years" instead of "for three years".

A native-speaking partner will spot these at once — and once someone has corrected you kindly, you rarely make the same mistake again.
